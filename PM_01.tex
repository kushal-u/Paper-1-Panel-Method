	\begin{abstract}
		Porous airfoils can modify aerodynamic forces through passive, pressure-driven flow between connected surface pores. This study develops a reduced-order aerodynamic–hydraulic framework for a NACA 0018 airfoil by coupling an inviscid source–vortex panel method with rectangular-channel flow models. Three configurations are examined: horizontal channels connecting chordwise locations on the same surface, vertical channels connecting the pressure and suction surfaces, and a combined arrangement of hydraulically independent channels. The predicted aerodynamic response depends strongly on channel orientation and connectivity. Horizontal channels redistribute surface pressure and increase the pressure-based lift coefficient at positive angles of attack, with larger channel heights producing stronger changes. Vertical channels promote pressure equalisation between the two surfaces and reduce the pressure-based lift relative to the solid airfoil. In the combined arrangement, the channels interact through the external pressure field, producing a response closer to the solid-airfoil baseline. Pressure and velocity difference fields indicate that flow modifications are concentrated near the surface pores. These findings highlight the importance of channel placement and connectivity in porous-airfoil design. The framework provides an efficient tool for preliminary screening, with the predicted inviscid trends requiring subsequent viscous simulation and experimental validation.
	\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Improving airfoil performance without relying on powered control systems remains an important objective in aerospace and energy applications. Conventional high-lift and flow-control devices can be effective, but they may introduce mechanical complexity, additional mass, maintenance requirements, and changes that disrupt the smooth external surface of the airfoil. Passive flow-control concepts provide an alternative strategy in which natural pressure differences around an aerodynamic body drive flow redistribution. Porous surfaces, permeable regions, localised pores, and internal channels employ this principle by permitting fluid transport between regions of unequal pressure. Their effectiveness, however, depends on pore placement, the connections between pores, and the strength of the interaction between the resulting surface-normal flow and the external aerodynamic field.

Porosity does not necessarily improve aerodynamic performance. Porosity extending along the entire airfoil chord, as well as other distributed porous arrangements, can reduce the rate of lift increase with angle of attack and produce competing effects on drag \cite{Mineck1996FullChordPorosity,Aldheeb2018PorousAirfoilsWings}. Experiments and numerical studies further show that performance depends on the location of the porous region and the permitted internal flow directions \cite{Hanna2019PorosityModerateRe,Zhu2023NACA0018PorousTrailingEdge,Li2023PartiallyPorousAirfoils}. These findings motivate the use of selectively connected pores to control pressure redistribution.

For an airfoil containing individual channels, the aerodynamic effect depends on which pressure regions are connected. A channel connecting pores on the same airfoil surface transfers fluid between different chordwise locations, whereas a channel connecting the pressure and suction surfaces permits flow between the two sides of the airfoil. These arrangements can produce different changes in surface pressure even when their pore areas are similar. Consequently, porosity alone does not specify the design: channel connectivity and resistance must also be represented. This distinction is particularly relevant when several channels operate simultaneously, because their influence on the external pressure field can alter the flow through neighbouring channels.


Thin-airfoil models describe steady and unsteady porous-airfoil behaviour by relating pressure differences to flow through distributed permeability \cite{Hajian2017PorosityGradients,Baddoo2021UnsteadyPorousAerofoils}, although accuracy can decrease for small porous regions or low flow resistance \cite{Luesutthiviboon2019ThinAirfoilAssessment}. These formulations provide efficient aerodynamic predictions but do not explicitly resolve airfoil thickness or finite-length channels connecting selected surface pores. Their local porous boundary conditions are useful for distributed permeable surfaces, whereas modelling individual channels requires the pressure at each connected pore and a relation between pressure difference, channel geometry, and flow rate.

Passive internal connections have already been investigated for aerodynamic control. Acarer reported improved peak lift-to-drag ratio using passive slots \cite{Acarer2020DU12W262PassiveFlow}, while Kestel et al. modified separated vortex flow through passive internal channels \cite{Kestel2020PassiveBleeding}. Bahadi et al. examined curved slots for improved performance at high angles of attack \cite{Bahadi2026CurvedPassiveSlot}. These studies establish the potential of pressure-driven internal flow. The present work builds on this principle by developing a general reduced-order framework for multiple pairs of pores connected by independent channels, enabling different arrangements to be evaluated consistently.

Porous-region optimisation has also been investigated for static-stall control \cite{Zamani2026NACA0015PorousStall}. For individually connected pores, the design variables additionally include pore locations, channel dimensions, and the choice of same-surface or cross-surface connections. Changes in channel length and height modify hydraulic resistance, while changes in pore position modify the available pressure difference. Both effects must therefore be considered together. Exploring these combinations solely through viscous simulations or experiments is costly, motivating a computationally efficient model for preliminary assessment before detailed analysis of selected configurations.

Potential-flow panel methods offer an efficient means of calculating surface pressures on airfoils of finite thickness without discretising the surrounding fluid domain \cite{HessSmith1967PotentialFlow,Hess1990PanelMethods,Birk2022HessSmithGuide}. In a two-dimensional source--vortex formulation, the source strengths enforce the surface-normal boundary condition, while circulation and the trailing-edge Kutta condition determine the lifting solution. This structure accommodates porous flow by replacing the impermeability condition locally with a prescribed pore-normal velocity. The resulting surface velocities determine the pressure differences driving the internal channel flow. A panel formulation therefore provides a direct connection between airfoil geometry, pore flow, and aerodynamic forces at a cost suitable for repeated design evaluations.

Although the panel equations are linear for prescribed pore velocities, the complete aero--hydraulic problem is generally nonlinear. Surface pressure depends quadratically on velocity, and changing the channel flow changes both the pore velocities and the pressures driving that flow. This feedback requires a consistent coupled solution even when a linear laminar hydraulic-resistance relation is used. Treating channel flow as a fixed input would omit this interaction. The present framework instead updates the internal flow and external pressures until the coupled equations satisfy the prescribed convergence criterion.

Established viscous--inviscid methods combine an outer-flow solution with boundary-layer equations to represent additional aerodynamic effects \cite{DrelaGiles1987ViscousInviscid}. XFOIL uses this approach with a panel-based inviscid solution and transition modelling \cite{Drela1989XFOIL}. Here, the external solver remains inviscid to isolate the aero--hydraulic interaction at low computational cost, and XFOIL provides a reference for the solid-airfoil baseline. This comparison assesses the external aerodynamic calculation; validation of the coupled porous model requires separate evidence for the internal flow and its interaction with the surrounding fluid.

The present study addresses the need for a reduced-order framework that explicitly represents finite-length channels between selected pairs of pores and couples their pressure-driven flow to the external aerodynamic solution. A two-dimensional source--vortex panel method with a Kutta condition is coupled iteratively to a hydraulic-resistance model for rectangular channels. Three NACA~0018 configurations are examined: four chordwise same-surface channels, four pressure-to-suction-surface channels, and a combined arrangement containing eight hydraulically independent channels. Channel heights of 8, 9, and 10~mm are assessed over angles of attack from $-5^{\circ}$ to $15^{\circ}$ at a chord Reynolds number of $5\times10^{5}$.

The combined arrangement permits the aerodynamic interaction between channel types to be assessed without introducing internal junctions. Although each channel is hydraulically independent, all channels influence a shared external pressure field. Comparing the three configurations therefore examines how their effects combine and whether connectivity changes the sensitivity to channel height and angle of attack.

The contribution is a consistent framework for comparing how channel connectivity affects internal flow, surface pressure, lift, pitching moment, and pressure-based streamwise force. The method supports preliminary design screening. Since it does not resolve boundary layers, skin friction, separation, or stall, its predictions are interpreted as inviscid trends requiring subsequent viscous simulation and experimental validation.

\section{Methods}
\label{sec:numerical_methodology}

\subsection{Modelling framework and assumptions}
\label{subsec:overall_framework}

A two-dimensional coupled aero--hydraulic framework is used to analyse
passive internal channels in a NACA~0018 airfoil. A \emph{pore} denotes
the surface region where a channel intersects the airfoil boundary,
whereas a \emph{channel} connects two pores through the airfoil interior.
The external flow is represented by a steady source--vortex panel method,
and each channel is described by a one-dimensional pressure-loss model
per unit span.

The external flow is assumed incompressible, inviscid and irrotational.
Boundary layers, skin friction, separation and stall are therefore not
resolved. The internal channels are straight, smooth and of constant
height, with sidewall effects neglected. Entrance turning, detailed
pore-mouth flow and jet mixing are represented only through the stated
modelling assumptions. The framework is intended for preliminary
comparison of channel layouts.

The external and internal models are coupled through pore pressures and
surface-normal velocities. Channel flow rates are adjusted until the two
models are consistent, as summarised in
Figure~\ref{fig:coupled_solution_workflow}.

\subsection{Airfoil and channel geometry}
\label{subsec:airfoil_channel_geometry}

The NACA~0018 airfoil is generated from the standard analytical profile
with a closed trailing edge \cite{Ladson1996NACAAirfoils}. A baseline of
$N_0=\text{[insert panel count]}$ cosine-spaced panels is used, with
additional boundaries inserted at the pore edges. Each of the resulting
$N$ panels lies entirely on a solid region or within a pore. Panel $i$
has length $S_i$, midpoint $(x_i,y_i)$, unit tangent $\boldsymbol t_i$
and outward unit normal $\boldsymbol n_i$.

Three configurations are considered:
\begin{enumerate}
	\item \textbf{Horizontal porous airfoil:} four independent horizontal
	channels, with two connecting pores on the upper surface and two
	connecting pores on the lower surface;
	\item \textbf{Vertical porous airfoil:} four independent vertical
	channels connecting the lower and upper surfaces at chordwise
	positions from approximately $x/c=0.10$ to $0.89$;
	\item \textbf{Combined porous airfoil:} all eight horizontal and
	vertical channels, with no internal exchange of flow between them.
\end{enumerate}
The layouts are shown in Figure~\ref{fig:porous_models}. Crossings in the
two-dimensional drawing do not create hydraulic connections; a physical
realisation of the combined layout would require separation of the
intersecting channels.

Each channel is defined by its two surface endpoints and height $H_k$.
The centreline length $L_k$ is the straight-line distance between these
endpoints. Each pore is defined by the intersection of the channel strip
with the airfoil surface. Before calculation, the geometry is checked
for pore overlap, minimum pore separation and containment of the
channels within the airfoil. Invalid geometries are excluded.

\subsection{External aerodynamic model}
\label{subsec:external_aerodynamic_model}

\subsubsection{Source--vortex panel formulation}

The panel method follows the boundary-integral representation of incompressible
potential flow developed by Hess and Smith and described in later panel-method
treatments \cite{HessSmith1967PotentialFlow,KatzPlotkin2001LowSpeedAerodynamics,
	Birk2022HessSmithGuide}. The velocity potential $\Phi$ satisfies
\begin{equation}
	\boldsymbol V=\nabla\Phi,
	\qquad \nabla^2\Phi=0,
	\label{eq:potential_flow}
\end{equation}
The external velocity is represented by the freestream, one constant source
strength $\lambda_j$ on every panel and one uniform vortex-sheet strength
$\gamma$ over the closed boundary. The sources enforce the prescribed normal velocity on the finite-thickness
geometry, while the vortex sheet provides circulation.

For a solid surface, the normal velocity at every control point is zero. At a
pore, this boundary condition is replaced by a prescribed normal transpiration
velocity, an approach consistent with reduced porous-surface formulations
\cite{Hajian2017PorosityGradients,Baddoo2021UnsteadyPorousAerofoils}:
\begin{equation}
	\boldsymbol V_i\boldsymbol\cdot\boldsymbol n_i=v_{n,i},
	\label{eq:transpiration_bc}
\end{equation}
where $v_{n,i}>0$ denotes flow out of the airfoil and $v_{n,i}<0$ denotes flow
into it. With the adopted influence-coefficient convention, the discrete normal
equation is
\begin{equation}
	\pi\lambda_i+
	\sum_{\substack{j=1\\j\neq i}}^N I_{ij}\lambda_j
	-\gamma\sum_{j=1}^N K_{ij}
	=-2\pi\boldsymbol V_\infty\boldsymbol\cdot\boldsymbol n_i+2\pi v_{n,i},
	\label{eq:panel_normal_equation}
\end{equation}
where $I_{ij}$ and $K_{ij}$ are the normal source and vortex influence
coefficients. The freestream vector is
$\boldsymbol V_\infty=V_\infty(\cos\alpha,\sin\alpha)$.
The first and last panels lie immediately adjacent to the trailing edge,
with oppositely directed tangents. The trailing-edge Kutta condition is
\begin{equation}
	V_{t,1}+V_{t,N}=0.
	\label{eq:kutta_condition}
\end{equation}
Equations~\eqref{eq:panel_normal_equation} and \eqref{eq:kutta_condition} form an
$(N+1)\times(N+1)$ linear system for
$[\lambda_1,\ldots,\lambda_N,\gamma]^{\mathrm T}$. Because the influence matrix
depends only on the final panel geometry, it is assembled and LU-factorised
once for each fixed pore-aligned discretisation. For that discretisation,
changes in angle of attack or pore-normal velocity affect only the
right-hand side.

\subsubsection{Surface velocity and pressure}

After solution of the panel system, the tangential velocity on panel $i$ is
\begin{equation}
	V_{t,i}=\boldsymbol V_\infty\boldsymbol\cdot\boldsymbol t_i
	+\frac{1}{2\pi}\sum_{j=1}^N\lambda_jJ_{ij}
	+\frac{\gamma}{2}
	-\frac{\gamma}{2\pi}\sum_{j=1}^N L_{ij},
	\label{eq:tangential_velocity}
\end{equation}
where $J_{ij}$ and $L_{ij}$ are the tangential source and vortex influence
coefficients. The local velocity is
$\boldsymbol V_i=V_{t,i}\boldsymbol t_i+v_{n,i}\boldsymbol n_i$.
Both velocity components enter the exterior pressure closure:


\begin{subequations}
	\label{eq:panel_pressure}
	\begin{align}
		C_{p,i}=1-\frac{V_{t,i}^2+v_{n,i}^2}{V_\infty^2}, \label{eq:panel_pressure_a} \\
		p_i=p_\infty+\frac{1}{2}\rho_\infty V_\infty^2C_{p,i}, \label{eq:panel_pressure_b}
	\end{align}
\end{subequations}
This expression is a potential-flow closure at the exterior surface; it does not
resolve the dissipative pore-mouth region.


\subsection{Pore velocity and pressure averaging}
\label{subsec:aero_hydraulic_coupling}

Let $q'_k$ denote the signed flow rate per unit span through channel $k$,
positive from pore 1 to pore 2. For pore $m$, the surface length is
\begin{equation}
	\ell_{m,k}=\sum_{i\in\mathcal I_{m,k}}S_i,
	\label{eq:pore_length}
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal I_{m,k}$ contains the panels within that pore. A uniform
normal velocity is imposed:
\begin{equation}
	v_{n,i}=\sigma_{m,k}\frac{q'_k}{\ell_{m,k}},
	\qquad i\in\mathcal I_{m,k},
	\label{eq:conservative_pore_mapping}
\end{equation}
with $\sigma_{1,k}=-1$, $\sigma_{2,k}=+1$ and zero normal velocity
on solid panels. This mapping conserves flow through each channel;
the inlet and outlet roles reverse when $q'_k$ changes sign.

The pore-normal velocity is based on the surface pore length, whereas
the mean channel velocity is based on the channel height. These
velocities therefore need not be equal. Panel pressures are calculated
from the local velocities before averaging over each pore:
\begin{equation}
	p_{m,k}=\frac{1}{\ell_{m,k}}
	\sum_{i\in\mathcal I_{m,k}}p_iS_i.
	\label{eq:pore_pressure_average}
\end{equation}
Averaging is restricted to pore panels. Because the prescribed normal
velocity is uniform within each pore, its squared value can be used
directly in the channel energy relation.


\subsection{Internal rectangular-channel model}
\label{subsec:internal_channel_model}

Each channel is represented by flow between parallel plates separated
by height $H_k$ over length $L_k$. The mean channel velocity, hydraulic
diameter and channel Reynolds number are
\begin{subequations}
	\label{eq:channel_kinematics}
	\begin{align}
		U_k &= \frac{q'_k}{H_k}, \\
		D_{h,k} &= 2H_k, \\
		Re_{h,k} &= \frac{\rho_\infty |U_k|D_{h,k}}{\mu_\infty}.
	\end{align}
\end{subequations}

Starting from Bernoulli's equation with pressure losses and neglecting
elevation differences, the following reduced-order channel relation
is adopted:
\begin{equation}
	p_{\mathrm{in},k}-p_{\mathrm{out},k}
	+\frac12\rho_\infty v_{n,\mathrm{in},k}^{2}
	=\Delta p_{f,k}+\frac12\rho_\infty U_k^2.
	\label{eq:channel_energy_closure}
\end{equation}
Here, $p_{\mathrm{in},k}$ and $p_{\mathrm{out},k}$ are the averaged
pressures at the higher- and lower-pressure pores, respectively, and
$v_{n,\mathrm{in},k}=|q'_k|/\ell_{\mathrm{in},k}$ is the inlet
pore-normal speed. The kinetic energy associated with external flow
parallel to the surface is assumed to be dissipated during entry.
The inlet normal contribution is retained, while the outlet contribution
is approximated using the mean channel velocity with a kinetic-energy
coefficient of unity.

For channels classified as laminar, the frictional pressure loss follows
the parallel-plate Hagen--Poiseuille relation
\cite{ShahLondon1978LaminarDucts}:
\begin{equation}
	\Delta p_{f,k}
	=\frac{12\mu_\infty L_k}{H_k^3}|q'_k|.
	\label{eq:laminar_channel_loss}
\end{equation}
For turbulent channels, the pressure loss is calculated using the
Darcy--Weisbach relation with the smooth-wall Blasius friction factor:
\begin{equation}
	\Delta p_{f,k}=f_k\frac{L_k}{D_{h,k}}
	\frac{\rho_\infty U_k^2}{2},
	\qquad f_k=0.3164Re_{h,k}^{-1/4}.
	\label{eq:turbulent_channel_loss}
\end{equation}
The Darcy friction-factor convention is used
\cite{Moody1944FrictionFactors}. Extending this turbulent correlation
to a parallel-plate channel through its hydraulic diameter is an
engineering approximation.

The laminar relation is applied for $Re_{h,k}\leq Re_{h,\mathrm{sw}}$
and the turbulent relation for $Re_{h,k}>Re_{h,\mathrm{sw}}$, where
$Re_{h,\mathrm{sw}}=\text{[insert value used in code]}$ is the prescribed
switching Reynolds number. No blending is applied, and physical
laminar--turbulent transition is not explicitly modelled. The frictional
loss is zero at zero flow.

For each external-flow update, the channel relation is solved for the
flow magnitude with the pore pressures and inlet normal-speed term
held fixed. The direction follows the static pore-pressure difference;
equal pressures give zero flow. A direct switch between the friction
relations can introduce a discontinuity, so a valid channel solution
must satisfy both the energy relation and the Reynolds-number condition
of the selected branch.

Both friction relations assume fully developed internal flow. Entrance
development and detailed flow at the pores are not resolved. Channel
Reynolds numbers and length ratios $L_k/D_{h,k}$ are used to assess the
scope of these approximations, as discussed in
Section~\ref{subsec:operating_conditions}.


\subsection{Nonlinearity and solution procedure}
\label{subsubsec:nonlinear_coupling}

The panel equations are linear for prescribed pore-normal velocities.
The coupled problem is nonlinear because channel flow changes the
external pressures that drive it. Nonlinearity arises from the squared
velocities in the external pressure and channel energy relations,
together with the nonlinear turbulent friction law. The laminar
Hagen--Poiseuille relation itself is linear.

All channel flow rates are solved simultaneously by seeking agreement
between the current flow estimates and the flows predicted by the
channel model. The scaled residual is
\begin{equation}
	R_k=\frac{q'_{k,\mathrm{est}}-q'_{k,\mathrm{hyd}}}
	{H_kV_\infty},
	\label{eq:coupling_residual}
\end{equation}
where $q'_{k,\mathrm{hyd}}$ is obtained from the channel model using
the current external solution. A Powell hybrid root solver seeks
$\boldsymbol R=\boldsymbol 0$, with a trust-region reflective
least-squares method used if required
\cite{MoreGarbowHillstrom1980MINPACK,Virtanen2020SciPy}.
Each residual evaluation applies the estimated flow rates at the pores,
solves the panel system and recalculates the pressure-driven channel
flows. Convergence requires the scaled mismatch to satisfy the
prescribed acceptance criterion.

Initial flow rates are estimated using solid-airfoil pressures and zero
inlet normal velocity. Nearby converged solutions initialise subsequent
cases where available. All cases reported in this study satisfy the
prescribed convergence criterion. Detailed numerical settings are
provided with the accompanying code.

% generated with the exact friction treatment and force definition here.


\subsection{Aerodynamic forces and pitching moment}
\label{subsec:aerodynamic_coefficients}

The modelled net force includes exterior pressure forces and the
reaction associated with internal channel flow. Let $\mathcal C$
denote the closed nominal airfoil contour, including the pore
cross-sections, and $\mathcal P$ the union of those cross-sections.
The normal points out of the enclosed airfoil volume. For steady flow,
neglecting body forces, exterior skin friction and viscous tractions
across the pores, the force per unit span is
\begin{equation}
	\boldsymbol F'=
	-\int_{\mathcal C}(p-p_\infty)\boldsymbol n\,\mathrm ds
	-\rho_\infty\int_{\mathcal P}\boldsymbol Vv_n\,\mathrm ds.
	\label{eq:device_force_balance}
\end{equation}
The second term accounts for momentum transport through the pores.
Together, the terms include the resultant internal-wall reaction;
internal-wall forces must therefore not be added again. Pressure
across a pore represents a control-surface traction rather than a
force on a solid surface.

Pressure and momentum flux are evaluated at the same exterior
interface using
$\boldsymbol V_i=V_{t,i}\boldsymbol t_i+v_{n,i}\boldsymbol n_i$.
The dimensionless contribution from panel $i$ is
\begin{equation}
	\mathrm d\boldsymbol C_i=
	-C_{p,i}\boldsymbol n_i\frac{S_i}{c}
	-\frac{2\chi_i\boldsymbol V_i v_{n,i}S_i}{V_\infty^2c},
	\label{eq:cartesian_pressure_forces}
\end{equation}
where $\chi_i=1$ on pore panels and zero on solid panels. With
$C_x=\sum_i\mathrm dC_{x,i}$ and $C_y=\sum_i\mathrm dC_{y,i}$,
\begin{align}
	C_L&=C_y\cos\alpha-C_x\sin\alpha,\nonumber\\
	C_{D,\mathrm{model}}&=C_x\cos\alpha+C_y\sin\alpha.
	\label{eq:lift_pressure_drag}
\end{align}
The $x$-axis extends from the leading edge to the trailing edge, and
$y$ is positive towards the upper surface. The quarter-chord pitching
moment coefficient, positive nose-up, is
\begin{equation}
	C_M=-\sum_{i=1}^{N}
	\frac{(x_i-0.25c)\,\mathrm dC_{y,i}-y_i\,\mathrm dC_{x,i}}{c}.
	\label{eq:pitching_moment}
\end{equation}

Pressure-only coefficients $C_{L,p}$, $C_{D,p}$ and $C_{M,p}$ can be
obtained by omitting the momentum-flux term and are distinguished from
the modelled net-force estimates. Exterior skin friction, separation,
jet mixing and the loss-generated wake remain unresolved.
Consequently, $C_{D,\mathrm{model}}$ is not a prediction of total
physical drag, and lift-to-drag improvements are not inferred from
this inviscid calculation.

	\section{Results and discussion}
	\label{sec:results_discussion}
	
	The aerodynamic behaviour of the three porous-airfoil configurations
	was investigated over an angle-of-attack range of $-5^\circ$ to
	$15^\circ$. Three pore heights, $H=8$, $9$ and $10~\mathrm{mm}$,
	were considered for the horizontal, vertical and horizontal--vertical
	porous airfoils. The corresponding impermeable (solid) airfoil
	panel-method solution and the inviscid XFOIL prediction are included as
	reference cases.
	
	The effect of the pores is first examined through the lift and
	moment coefficients over the angle-of-attack range. The observed aerodynamic trends are then
	interpreted using the corresponding pressure and velocity field
	differences. A detailed flow-field comparison is presented at
	$\alpha=10^\circ$ for a pore height of $H=9~\mathrm{mm}$.
	
	
	% ============================================================
	% BASELINE VALIDATION
	% ============================================================
	
	\subsection{Panel method validation against XFOIL}
	\label{subsec:Panel_method_validation_against_XFOIL}
	
	The impermeable source--vortex panel-method solution is compared with
	the corresponding inviscid XFOIL prediction in
	Figures.~\ref{fig:cl_aoa_three_models} and
	\ref{fig:cm_aoa_three_models}. Both methods show similar trends in lift
	and pitching moment over the investigated angle-of-attack range.
	
	This agreement provides a useful reference for assessing the aerodynamic
	effects introduced by the pores. Small differences between the
	panel-method and XFOIL predictions are expected because the two
	approaches use different numerical formulations, although both represent
	inviscid external flow.
	
	The impermeable panel-method solution is therefore used as the primary
	reference when evaluating the porous-airfoil configurations. This
	ensures that the porous and solid airfoils are compared using the same
	geometry, panel discretisation, aerodynamic formulation and operating
	conditions.

	\subsection{Effect of pores on lift}
	\label{subsec:lift_results}
	
	Figure~\ref{fig:cl_aoa_three_models} compares the variation of lift
	coefficient with angle of attack for the three porous-airfoil
	configurations and pore heights.
	\begin{figure*}
		\centering
		\includegraphics[
		width=0.96\textwidth
		]{figs/CL_vs_AoA_three_models_three_diameters.png}
		\caption{
			Lift coefficient as a function of angle of attack for
			(a) the horizontal porous airfoil,
			(b) the vertical porous airfoil, and
			(c) the horizontal--vertical porous airfoil.
			Results are shown for pore heights
			$H=8$, $9$ and $10~\mathrm{mm}$ together with the
			impermeable panel-method and inviscid XFOIL reference
			solutions.
		}
		\label{fig:cl_aoa_three_models}
	\end{figure*}
	The horizontal porous airfoil produces the largest change in lift.
	At positive angles of attack, its lift coefficient is higher than that
	of the impermeable reference, and the difference becomes more
	pronounced as the angle of attack increases. The effect also strengthens
	with pore height, with $H=10~\mathrm{mm}$ producing the highest lift
	at larger positive angles of attack. At negative angles of attack,
	increasing the pore height similarly increases the magnitude of the
	negative lift.This nearly symmetric lift response about the zero-lift condition is
	consistent with the symmetric NACA~0018 geometry.
	
	For the horizontal configuration, both ends of each pore are located on
	the same airfoil surface. The streamwise variation in surface pressure
	therefore creates a pressure difference between the two ends and drives
	internal flow from the locally higher-pressure region towards the
	lower-pressure region. This produces suction at one end and blowing at
	the other, changing the local surface-normal velocity and redistributing
	the external pressure and velocity fields along the airfoil surface.
	At positive angle of attack, the different pressure distributions over
	the suction and pressure surfaces lead to a net redistribution of
	aerodynamic loading that increases lift.
	
	The stronger dependence on pore height is consistent with the reduction
	in hydraulic resistance as the pore becomes larger. For a given pressure
	difference, a larger pore height permits a greater internal flow and
	therefore a stronger interaction between the pore flow and the external
	aerodynamic field.
	
	The vertical porous airfoil shows a distinctly different behaviour.
	At positive angles of attack, its lift coefficient is generally lower
	than that of the impermeable airfoil, while the three pore-height cases
	remain relatively close over most of the investigated range.
	
	In this configuration, each pore connects the  pressure
	surface to the suction surface. The resulting pressure
	difference drives internal flow between the two surfaces and partially
	reduces the pressure difference responsible for lift. This pressure
	equalisation is consistent with the lower lift coefficient obtained for
	the vertical porous airfoil. The small separation between the
	pore-height cases also indicates that this configuration is less
	sensitive to pore height than the horizontal arrangement.
	
	The horizontal--vertical porous airfoil remains comparatively close to
	the impermeable reference. At positive angles of attack, its lift
	coefficient is generally slightly lower than that of the solid airfoil,
	and the three pore-height cases remain closely grouped.
	
	This behaviour reflects the simultaneous influence of the horizontal
	and vertical pore arrangements. The horizontal pores redistribute flow
	between different chordwise locations on the same surface, whereas the
	vertical pores transfer flow between the pressure and suction surfaces.
	The lift-enhancing redistribution associated with the horizontal pores
	is therefore partly offset by the pressure equalisation introduced by
	the vertical pores.
	
	The horizontal and vertical pores remain physically separate and
	hydraulically independent. However, their aerodynamic effects interact
	through the external pressure field, since changes produced by one set
	of pores alter the surface pressures that drive the other set.
	Consequently, the response of the horizontal--vertical porous airfoil is
	not a simple superposition of the two individual configurations.
	
	Overall, the results show that the aerodynamic effect of the pores is
	governed mainly by their orientation, placement and the pressure
	difference between the connected surface locations. Pore height also
	influences the response, but its effect is strongest for the horizontal
	configuration and considerably weaker for the vertical and
	horizontal--vertical arrangements.

	\subsection{Effect of pores on pitching moment}
	\label{subsec:moment_results}
	
	The corresponding moment coefficients are
	presented in Figure.~\ref{fig:cm_aoa_three_models}.
	\begin{figure*}
		\centering
		
		\includegraphics[
		width=0.96\textwidth
		]{figs/CM_vs_AoA_three_models_three_diameters.png}
		
		\caption{
			Pitching-moment coefficient about the quarter chord as a
			function of angle of attack for
			(a) the horizontal porous airfoil,
			(b) the vertical porous airfoil, and
			(c) the horizontal--vertical porous airfoil.
			Results are shown for pore heights
			$H=8$, $9$ and $10~\mathrm{mm}$ together with the
			impermeable panel-method and inviscid XFOIL reference solutions.
		}
		
		\label{fig:cm_aoa_three_models}
	\end{figure*}
	The horizontal porous airfoil produces the largest increase from the
	impermeable-airfoil response. As the positive angle of attack
	increases, the pitching-moment coefficient becomes progressively more
	negative. The magnitude of this change also increases with pore height,
	with $H=10~\mathrm{mm}$ producing the largest negative pitching moment
	at higher positive angles of attack.
	
	This behaviour is associated with the chordwise redistribution of
	surface pressure caused by the horizontal pores. Since the two ends
	of each pore are located at different chordwise positions along a given
	airfoil surface, the pressure changes induced by the internal flow act
	at different distances from the quarter-chord reference point. These
	local pressure variations therefore contribute differently to the
	pitching moment. Increasing the pore height strengthens the internal
	flow and the resulting pressure redistribution, leading to a larger
	change in the pitching-moment coefficient.
	
	The increase in lift obtained with the horizontal porous airfoil is
	therefore accompanied by a larger negative pitching moment. This
	represents an important aerodynamic trade-off, since the configuration
	that provides the greatest lift enhancement also produces the largest
	change in longitudinal loading.
	
	The vertical porous airfoil produces considerably smaller changes in
	moment, and the three pore-height cases remain closely grouped
	over most of the investigated angle-of-attack range. This behaviour is
	consistent with the weaker dependence on pore height observed in the
	lift results.
	
	For the vertical configuration, the two ends of each pore are positioned
	on the upper and lower surfaces at approximately the same chordwise
	location. The resulting pressure redistribution therefore occurs mainly
	between the pressure and suction surfaces rather than between widely
	separated positions along the chord. The corresponding change in the chordwise pressure distribution is
	smaller than for the horizontal pores, resulting in a weaker
	pitching-moment response.
	
	The horizontal--vertical porous airfoil exhibits behaviour between that
	of the individual horizontal and vertical configurations. The pitching
	moment becomes increasingly negative with positive angle of attack,
	although the departure from the impermeable-airfoil solution remains
	smaller than for the horizontal porous airfoil. The influence of pore
	height also becomes more noticeable at higher positive angles of attack.
	
	In this configuration, the horizontal pores redistribute loading
	between different chordwise positions, while the vertical pores mainly
	redistribute pressure between the upper and lower surfaces. The two pore
	arrangements remain physically separate and hydraulically independent,
	but their aerodynamic effects interact through the external pressure
	field. The resulting pitching-moment variation therefore reflects the
	combined influence of these two pressure-redistribution mechanisms.
	
	Overall, the pitching-moment results show that the effect of the pores
	depends not only on the extent of the pressure redistribution, but also
	on where it occurs along the chord. The horizontal porous airfoil
	produces the largest change in moment because the associated
	pressure variations occur at widely separated chordwise locations,
	creating a stronger moment about the quarter-chord reference point.
	In contrast, the vertical porous airfoil produces a smaller change
	because its pressure redistribution occurs mainly between the upper
	and lower surfaces at similar chordwise positions.

	\subsection{Flow field characteristics of porous airfoils}
	\label{subsec:flowfield_results}
	
	To examine how the pores alter the external flow, the pressure and
	velocity fields of the porous airfoils are compared with those of the
	corresponding impermeable airfoil at $\alpha=10^\circ$ and
	$H=9~\mathrm{mm}$.
	
	The comparison is presented using pressure- and velocity-difference
	contours rather than the absolute flow fields. The absolute contours
	are dominated by the overall aerodynamic behaviour of the airfoil and
	therefore make the local influence of the pores more difficult to
	identify. Subtracting the impermeable-airfoil solution isolates the
	changes introduced by the pore flow, allowing the location, direction
	and relative strength of the resulting pressure and velocity variations
	to be examined more clearly.

	\subsubsection{Pressure field characteristics}
	
	Figure~\ref{fig:pressure_difference} presents the pressure difference
	between the porous and impermeable airfoils,
	
	\begin{equation}
		\Delta p
		=
		p_{\mathrm{porous}}
		-
		p_{\mathrm{solid}},
		\label{eq:pressure_field_difference}
	\end{equation}
	
	for the three pore arrangements.
	
	\begin{figure*}
		\centering
		\includegraphics[
		width=0.62\textwidth
		]{figs/pressure_difference_pa_portrait_one_colorbar.png}
		\caption{{\normalfont\normalsize
				Pressure-field difference between the porous and
				impermeable airfoils at $\alpha=10^\circ$ and
				$H=9~\mathrm{mm}$ for
				(a) the horizontal porous airfoil,
				(b) the vertical porous airfoil, and
				(c) the horizontal--vertical porous airfoil.

				to the impermeable airfoil, while negative values indicate
				a reduction.
		}}

	The pressure difference is shown instead of the absolute pressure field
	to isolate the changes introduced by the pores. In the absolute
	solution, the pressure distribution is dominated by the overall
	airfoil loading and the strong pressure gradients near the leading
	edge. Subtracting the impermeable-airfoil solution makes the local
	changes associated with the pore flow easier to identify.
	
	The strongest pressure differences occur near the pore ends, where the
	internal flow directly influences the surrounding external flow. In
	several locations, neighbouring positive and negative values of
	$\Delta p$ form a dipole-like pattern. This is associated with suction
	at one end of a pore and blowing at the other, producing local regions
	of reduced and increased pressure relative to the impermeable airfoil.
	
	The effect of these pressure changes on lift depends on where they occur
	on the airfoil. At positive angle of attack, a reduction in pressure on
	the upper suction surface strengthens the suction and contributes to
	higher lift, whereas an increase in pressure has the opposite effect.
	On the lower pressure surface, an increase in pressure contributes
	positively to lift, while a pressure reduction tends to decrease it.
	
	For the horizontal porous airfoil, the pressure differences are mainly
	concentrated around the chordwise pore ends. Because both ends of each
	pore lie on the same airfoil surface, the internal flow redistributes
	pressure between different chordwise locations. The combined pressure
	changes on the upper and lower surfaces increase the pressure difference
	across the airfoil, consistent with the higher lift coefficient observed
	for this configuration.
	
	The vertical porous airfoil produces a different pattern because each
	pore connects the pressure surface to the suction surface. Flow is
	therefore driven from the higher-pressure region towards the
	lower-pressure region, producing local pressure changes at both pore
	ends. At the same time, this cross-surface flow partially reduces the
	pressure difference between the two surfaces, which is consistent with
	the lower lift coefficient obtained for the vertical configuration.
	
	The horizontal--vertical porous airfoil contains features associated
	with both pore arrangements. The horizontal pores redistribute pressure
	along the airfoil surfaces, while the vertical pores transfer flow
	between the pressure and suction surfaces. Although the two pore systems
	remain physically separate and hydraulically independent, their
	aerodynamic effects interact through the external pressure field.
	Consequently, the pressure equalisation associated with the vertical
	pores partly counteracts the pressure redistribution produced by the
	horizontal pores, giving an overall aerodynamic behaviour closer to
	that of the impermeable airfoil.

	\begin{figure*}

	The velocity field is presented as the percentage difference relative
	to the impermeable-airfoil solution,
	
	\begin{equation}
		\Delta |\boldsymbol{V}|_{\%}
		=
		\frac{
			|\boldsymbol{V}|_{\mathrm{porous}}
			-
			|\boldsymbol{V}|_{\mathrm{solid}}

		}
		\times 100.
		\label{eq:velocity_field_difference}
	\end{equation}
	
	This comparison isolates the changes introduced by the pores from the dominant background flow around the airfoil. The largest velocity differences occur close to the pore ends,
	where the imposed surface-normal flow locally perturbs the external
	velocity field. Adjacent regions of acceleration and deceleration are
	visible around several pores and correspond closely to the pressure
	patterns shown in Figure.~\ref{fig:pressure_difference}.
	
	The velocity and pressure fields show the expected inverse relationship
	described by Bernoulli's equation. Regions where the flow accelerates
	are generally associated with a reduction in static pressure, whereas
	regions of lower velocity correspond to higher static pressure. The
	velocity contours therefore help explain how the pore-induced changes
	in the local flow field lead to the observed pressure redistribution
	and, consequently, changes in the aerodynamic forces and pitching
	moment.
	
	For the horizontal porous airfoil, alternating regions of acceleration
	and deceleration are concentrated around the chordwise pore ends. Since
	both ends of each pore lie on the same airfoil surface, the internal
	flow redistributes the external velocity between different chordwise
	locations. The combined changes on the suction and pressure surfaces
	strengthen the pressure difference across the airfoil, consistent with
	the increase in lift and the larger change in pitching moment observed
	for this configuration.
	
	The vertical porous airfoil shows a different velocity pattern. Flow
	between the pressure and suction surfaces changes the external velocity
	on both sides of the airfoil and reduces part of the velocity contrast
	between them. This behaviour is consistent with the partial pressure
	equalisation discussed previously and the corresponding reduction in
	lift. A broader region of velocity difference is also visible near the
	leading edge, where the external flow experiences strong pressure and
	velocity gradients.
	
	The horizontal--vertical porous airfoil contains features associated
	with both pore arrangements. The horizontal pores produce local
	chordwise acceleration and deceleration, while the vertical pores
	redistribute flow between the pressure and suction surfaces. Although
	the two pore arrangements remain physically separate and hydraulically
	independent, their aerodynamic effects interact through the external
	flow field. The resulting velocity distribution therefore reflects the
	combined influence of both mechanisms rather than a simple
	superposition of the individual configurations.
	
	Overall, the velocity contours support the trends observed in the
	pressure field and aerodynamic coefficients. The horizontal pores
	primarily redistribute the flow along the airfoil surfaces, whereas the
	vertical pores transfer flow between the pressure and suction surfaces.
	These different flow mechanisms contribute to the contrasting lift and
	pitching-moment behaviour of the three porous-airfoil configurations.
	

	
	\section{Conclusion and future work}
	\label{sec:conclusion}
	
	A reduced-order aero--hydraulic framework has been developed to investigate
	NACA~0018 airfoils containing pressure-driven internal channels. The external
	flow is represented using a source--vortex panel method and is coupled to
	finite-length rectangular-channel models through the pore-normal velocity.
	The main conclusions of the present study are:
	
	\begin{itemize}
		
		\item The aerodynamic response of a porous airfoil depends strongly on
		the orientation, location and connectivity of the internal channels.
		Porosity alone is therefore insufficient to describe the resulting
		aerodynamic behaviour.
		
		\item Horizontal chordwise channels produce the strongest aerodynamic
		modification among the configurations considered. By connecting
		different chordwise locations on the same airfoil surface, these channels
		redistribute pressure without directly equalising the pressure and
		suction surfaces.
		
		\item At positive angles of attack, the horizontal configuration
		increases lift relative to the impermeable airfoil. The magnitude of this
		effect increases with channel height, demonstrating the sensitivity of
		the aerodynamic response to the hydraulic resistance of the internal
		passages.
		
		\item The lift enhancement produced by the horizontal channels is
		accompanied by a more negative pitching moment. This demonstrates that
		improvements in lift cannot be considered independently of changes in
		longitudinal aerodynamic loading.
		
		\item Vertical channels exhibit a fundamentally different behaviour.
		Connecting the pressure and suction surfaces allows pressure-driven flow
		between the two sides of the airfoil, producing partial pressure
		equalisation and consequently reducing lift relative to the impermeable
		configuration at positive angles of attack.
		
		\item The combined horizontal--vertical configuration does not behave as
		a simple superposition of the two individual arrangements. Although the
		channel systems are hydraulically independent, they interact through the
		external pressure field. The pressure equalisation associated with the
		vertical channels therefore partly offsets the lift-enhancing effect of
		the horizontal channels.
		
		\item Pressure- and velocity-difference fields show that the strongest
		aerodynamic modifications occur locally around the channel openings.
		Suction and blowing generated by the internal flow alter the surrounding
		external velocity and pressure fields, which subsequently modify the
		integrated aerodynamic forces and pitching moment.
		
		\item The results demonstrate that the pressure difference between
		connected surface locations is a key driver of porous-airfoil behaviour.
		Channel placement, orientation and height should therefore be considered
		simultaneously when designing internal porous networks.
		
		\item The developed framework provides a computationally inexpensive
		method for screening multiple internal-channel configurations before
		progressing to substantially more expensive viscous CFD simulations or
		experimental investigations.
		
	\end{itemize}
	
	The present results should be interpreted as inviscid aerodynamic trends.
	The external panel method does not resolve boundary layers, transition,
	skin-friction drag, flow separation or stall, and the pressure-based
	streamwise-force coefficient should therefore not be interpreted as the
	total drag of the porous airfoil. These limitations define the next stage
	of the investigation.
	
	\subsection{Future work}
	
	Future development will focus on:
	
	\begin{itemize}
		
		\item incorporating a viscous boundary-layer model into the panel-method
		framework to estimate skin-friction drag, transition and the influence
		of viscous--inviscid interaction;
		
		\item extending the design space beyond the three prescribed
		configurations by optimising channel location, orientation, height and
		connectivity to identify improved internal porous-network topologies;
		
		\item investigating selected configurations using higher-fidelity
		unsteady CFD to resolve pore-mouth flow, boundary-layer interaction,
		separation and wake dynamics that cannot be represented by the present
		reduced-order formulation;
		
		\item validating the predicted pressure redistribution and aerodynamic
		trends experimentally using manufactured porous-airfoil models; and
		
		\item extending the analysis towards three-dimensional porous wings and
		application-relevant operating conditions once the underlying
		aero--hydraulic mechanisms have been established.
		
	\end{itemize}
	
	The present framework therefore provides the first stage of a hierarchical
	design process in which rapid reduced-order screening is used to identify
	promising internal-channel arrangements for subsequent optimisation,
	high-fidelity viscous simulation and experimental validation.
