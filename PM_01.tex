	\begin{abstract}
	Passive, pressure-driven flow between connected surface pores can modify aerodynamic forces without external actuation. This study develops a reduced-order aerodynamic–hydraulic framework for a NACA 0018 airfoil by coupling an inviscid source–vortex panel method with rectangular-channel flow models. Three configurations are investigated: horizontal channels connecting chordwise locations on the same surface, vertical channels connecting the lower and upper surfaces, and a combined arrangement of hydraulically independent channels. Aerodynamic coefficients are obtained by integrating the predicted surface pressure. At positive angles of attack, horizontal channels increase lift through pressure redistribution, with larger channel heights producing greater increases in lift. Vertical channels reduce the pressure difference between the two surfaces and decrease lift relative to the impermeable baseline. In the combined configuration, the channels interact through the external pressure field, yielding a response closer to that of the solid airfoil. Pressure and velocity difference fields show that the strongest flow modifications occur near the pore openings. These results identify channel orientation and pore connections as key factors governing the aerodynamic response of porous airfoils. The framework supports preliminary design screening and provides inviscid predictions for subsequent assessment using viscous simulations and experiments.
	
	\end{abstract}

	\section{Introduction}
	\label{sec:introduction}

Porous wings have attracted attention due to their favourable acoustic properties \cite{Zhu2023NACA0018PorousTrailingEdge}. Beyond acoustic applications, airfoils with anisotropic permeability have been considered for aerodynamic benefits, including lift and efficiency enhancement \cite{Hanna2019PorosityModerateRe,Li2023PartiallyPorousAirfoils}. 
Due to the multiscale nature of these flows, the design of porous wings remains challenging. 
This study addresses this challenge through a computationally inexpensive, extended panel-method formulation.

Previous porous-airfoil studies have reported reduced lift and either increased or decreased drag compared with solid airfoils \cite{Mineck1996FullChordPorosity,Aldheeb2018PorousAirfoilsWings}. 
Experimental and numerical evidence shows that aerodynamic performance depends on the location of the porous region and the permitted internal flow directions \cite{Hanna2019PorosityModerateRe,Zhu2023NACA0018PorousTrailingEdge,Li2023PartiallyPorousAirfoils}. 
These findings motivate the anisotropic and heterogeneous pore configurations examined in the present study. 
Here, anisotropy refers to the permitted directions of internal flow imposed by the channels. 
Heterogeneity refers to the spatial distribution of permeable pores and impermeable regions over the airfoil surface.
	
Thin airfoil theory provides efficient predictions of steady and unsteady porous airfoil behaviour \cite{Hajian2017PorosityGradients,Baddoo2021UnsteadyPorousAerofoils}.
These models describe how the pressure difference between the lower and upper airfoil surfaces drives flow through the porous regions.
Predicted surface pressures may differ from measurements when the porous region covers only a small part of the chord or offers little resistance to flow \cite{Luesutthiviboon2019ThinAirfoilAssessment}.
These models neglect airfoil thickness and do not explicitly describe internal channels connecting selected surface pores. Representing such channels requires the pressure at both pores and a relation linking channel dimensions and pressure difference to flow rate.

Passive internal channels transfer fluid between surface locations through pressure differences. 
Selected slot geometries can increase the peak lift-to-drag ratio and improve aerodynamic performance at high angles of attack \cite{Acarer2020DU12W262PassiveFlow,Bahadi2026CurvedPassiveSlot}. 
This internal flow can also alter separated vortex structures \cite{Kestel2020PassiveBleeding}.
However, this study focuses on specific geometries. 
A general reduced-order model is needed to compare multiple independent channels connecting selected pairs of surface pores.


Porous region optimisation has been investigated for static-stall control \cite{Zamani2026NACA0015PorousStall}. 
For airfoils with individual channels, the design must also specify which pores are connected and the dimensions and orientation of each channel. 
Channel length and height govern hydraulic resistance, while pore locations affect the driving pressure difference. 
These variables must therefore be assessed together. 
Evaluating their combinations through viscous simulations or experiments is costly, motivating an efficient model for preliminary design.

Panel methods efficiently calculates surface velocities and pressures while accounting for airfoil thickness. Only the airfoil surface is discretised, avoiding a mesh of the surrounding fluid \cite{HessSmith1967PotentialFlow,Hess1990PanelMethods,Birk2022HessSmithGuide}. 
In a two dimensional source--vortex formulation, the source strengths and circulation are determined by imposing the surface normal velocity condition and the trailing-edge Kutta condition. 
A prescribed normal velocity represents flow through each pore, while pressure differences between connected pores drive the internal channel flow. 
This links the aerodynamic and hydraulic models within an efficient computational framework. 
The external solver remains inviscid to examine the aero--hydraulic interaction at low computational cost. 
XFOIL provides a reference for the solid-airfoil baseline \cite{Drela1989XFOIL}, while the coupled porous model requires separate validation.

A reduced-order framework is therefore needed to represent individual internal channels and their interaction with the external flow. 
The present study couples a two-dimensional source--vortex panel method to a hydraulic-resistance model. 
Each channel connects a selected pair of surface pores, with its flow determined by the pressure difference between them. This approach allows different pore connections to be assessed within a common aerodynamic model.

Three channel layouts are examined for a NACA~0018 airfoil. 
The horizontal layout contains four channels connecting pores at different chordwise locations on the same surface. 
The vertical layout contains four channels connecting the lower and upper surfaces. 
The combined layout includes all eight channels as hydraulically independent connections that interact through the external pressure field. 
Channel heights of 8, 9, and 10~mm are assessed over angles of attack from $-5^{\circ}$ to $15^{\circ}$ at a chord Reynolds number of $5\times10^{5}$.

The contribution is a coupled aero--hydraulic model of channels connecting selected pore pairs. Each channel is hydraulically independent.
This allows different channel connections to be compared using the same aerodynamic formulation.
The framework relates channel geometry and connectivity to internal flow, surface pressure, lift, and pitching moment. 
It is intended for rapid preliminary design screening. Since boundary layers, skin friction, separation, and stall are not resolved, the predictions represent inviscid trends requiring subsequent viscous simulation and experimental validation.



	
	
\section{Coupled aero-hydraulic methodology}
\label{sec:methodology}

\subsection{Model overview}
\label{subsec:model_overview}

The framework couples the external flow around the airfoil to internal flow through channels connecting selected surface pores. 
Each channel is hydraulically independent.
The channels interact through the external pressure field, while their flows impose suction and blowing at the pores.

The solution procedure is summarised in Fig.~\ref{fig:coupled_methodology}.
For each geometry, the panel matrix is factorised once.
The effect of unit flow through each channel is then precomputed. 
These coefficients allow surface velocities and pore pressures to be evaluated for trial channel flows without repeating the panel solve.
This step is exact within the discretised aerodynamic model.

Initial channel flows are estimated from the impermeable-airfoil pressure field.
At each iteration, the hydraulic model predicts the flows supported by the available pressure heads.
The differences between the trial and predicted flows define the residuals.
A damped Newton method updates all channel flows simultaneously \cite{NocedalWright2006NumericalOptimization}.
The Jacobian is evaluated analytically.

The final candidate is checked independently using a full panel solve and the original hydraulic model.
Acceptance requires a sufficiently small flow mismatch, a consistent energy balance and an admissible flow direction.
The accepted surface pressures are then integrated to calculate the lift, pressure-drag and pitching-moment coefficients.

\subsection{External source--vortex panel formulation}
\label{subsec:external_panel}

The external flow is assumed steady, incompressible, inviscid and irrotational in the fluid domain.
The velocity is expressed using a potential $\Phi$, such that $\mathbf{V}=\nabla\Phi$.
Incompressibility requires $\nabla\cdot\mathbf{V}=0$.
The potential therefore satisfies Laplace's equation
\cite{KatzPlotkin2001LowSpeedAerodynamics}:
\begin{equation}
	\nabla^{2}\Phi
	=
	\frac{\partial^{2}\Phi}{\partial x^{2}}
	+
	\frac{\partial^{2}\Phi}{\partial y^{2}}
	=0.
	\label{eq:laplace_equation}
\end{equation}
For a freestream speed $V_{\infty}$ and angle of attack $\alpha$, the freestream velocity and potential are
\begin{equation}
	\mathbf{V}_{\infty}
	=
	V_{\infty}
	\begin{bmatrix}
		\cos\alpha\\
		\sin\alpha
	\end{bmatrix},
	\qquad
	\Phi_{\infty}
	=
	V_{\infty}(x\cos\alpha+y\sin\alpha).
	\label{eq:freestream_velocity}
\end{equation}
The far-field condition is $\mathbf{V}\rightarrow\mathbf{V}_{\infty}$.
The linearity of Laplace's equation allows the external flow to be represented by superposing the freestream with source and vortex distributions.

The airfoil boundary is discretised into $N$ straight panels, ordered clockwise.
Boundary conditions are imposed at the panel midpoints.
Each panel carries a constant source strength $\lambda_j$.
A uniform vortex-sheet strength $\gamma$ is shared by all panels and permits nonzero circulation.
This follows the source--vortex panel formulation \cite{HessSmith1967PotentialFlow,Hess1990PanelMethods}.

The total external velocity at position $\mathbf{r}$ is
\begin{equation}
	\mathbf{V}(\mathbf{r})
	=
	\nabla\Phi
	=
	\mathbf{V}_{\infty}
	+
	\sum_{j=1}^{N}
	\left[
	\lambda_j\mathbf{v}^{(s)}_j(\mathbf{r})
	+
	\gamma\mathbf{v}^{(v)}_j(\mathbf{r})
	\right].
	\label{eq:total_external_velocity}
\end{equation}
Here, $\mathbf{v}^{(s)}_j$ and $\mathbf{v}^{(v)}_j$ are the velocities induced by unit-strength source
and vortex distributions on panel $j$.
Their signs follow the convention used below.

At collocation point $i$, the normal boundary condition is $\mathbf{V}_i\cdot\mathbf{n}_i=V_{n,i}$, where $\mathbf{n}_i$ is the outward unit normal.
The prescribed velocity $V_{n,i}$ is zero on impermeable panels. 
At a channel opening, it represents the panel-average normal velocity caused by the internal flow.
Positive values indicate blowing and negative values indicate suction.
The normal-velocity equation is
\begin{equation}
	\frac{\lambda_i}{2}
	+\frac{1}{2\pi}
	\sum_{\substack{j=1\\j\ne i}}^{N}
	I_{ij}\lambda_j
	-\frac{\gamma}{2\pi}
	\sum_{j=1}^{N}K_{ij}
	=
	V_{n,i}-V_{\infty}\cos\beta_i.
	\label{eq:normal_panel_equation}
\end{equation}
Here, $\beta_i$ is the angle measured from the freestream direction to the outward panel normal.
The tangential velocity, measured along the clockwise panel direction, is
\begin{equation}
	V_{t,i}
	=
	V_{\infty}\sin\beta_i
	+\frac{1}{2\pi}
	\sum_{j=1}^{N}J_{ij}\lambda_j
	+\frac{\gamma}{2}
	-\frac{\gamma}{2\pi}
	\sum_{j=1}^{N}L_{ij}.
	\label{eq:tangential_panel_velocity}
\end{equation}
The coefficients $I_{ij}$ and $J_{ij}$ describe the normal and tangential source influences.
The corresponding vortex coefficients are $K_{ij}$ and $L_{ij}$.
They are evaluated by analytical integration over each straight panel.
Their diagonal entries are set to zero.
The nonzero self-influence terms are included explicitly as $\lambda_i/2$ and $\gamma/2$.

The $N$ normal-velocity equations are supplemented by a trailing-edge Kutta condition.
For the adopted boundary ordering,
\begin{equation}
	V_{t,1}+V_{t,N}=0,
	\label{eq:kutta_condition}
\end{equation}
where panels 1 and $N$ meet at the trailing edge.
Together, these equations determine the source and vortex strengths.

For $M$ channels, the flow vector is $\mathbf{q}=[q'_1,\ldots,q'_M]^T$, where $q'_k$ is the flow rate per unit span through
channel $k$.
The prescribed normal velocities satisfy
\begin{equation}
	\mathbf{V}_n=\mathbf{M}\mathbf{q},
	\label{eq:normal_velocity_map}
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{M}$ is the conservative transfer operator defined in Section~\ref{subsec:interface_transfer}.

The aerodynamic unknowns are collected in 
$\mathbf{x}=[\lambda_1,\ldots,\lambda_N,\gamma]^T$.
Multiplying the normal-velocity and Kutta equations
by $2\pi$ gives
\begin{equation}
	\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}
	=
	\mathbf{b}_{\infty}+\mathbf{B}\mathbf{q},
	\qquad
	\mathbf{B}
	=
	\begin{bmatrix}
		2\pi\mathbf{M}\\
		\mathbf{0}_{1\times M}
	\end{bmatrix}.
	\label{eq:aero_matrix}
\end{equation}
The matrix $\mathbf{A}$ contains the panel influence coefficients and the Kutta equation.
The vector $\mathbf{b}_{\infty}$ contains the freestream contributions.
The zero final row of $\mathbf{B}$ indicates that channel flow does not directly enter the right-hand side of the Kutta equation.

For prescribed channel flows, the aerodynamic system is linear. 
It is solved using an LU factorisation, which is reused for a fixed panel geometry.
The resulting source and vortex strengths give the tangential velocities through Eq.~\eqref{eq:tangential_panel_velocity}.

Bernoulli's equation gives the pressure coefficient from the total local speed
\cite{KatzPlotkin2001LowSpeedAerodynamics}:
\begin{equation}
	C_{p,i}
	=
	1-\frac{V_{t,i}^{2}+V_{n,i}^{2}}{V_{\infty}^{2}}.
	\label{eq:pressure_coefficient}
\end{equation}
Both velocity components are retained because the normal velocity can be nonzero at a pore opening.
The panel pressure follows from
$p_i=p_{\infty}+\tfrac{1}{2}\rho V_{\infty}^{2}C_{p,i}$.


\subsection{Internal hydraulic model}
\label{subsec:internal_hydraulics}

Each straight channel has height $H_k$ and centreline
length $L_k$, measured between its surface endpoints.
Sidewall effects are neglected.
The internal flow is represented between parallel walls
using a two-dimensional, per-unit-span formulation.
The signed flow rate $q'_k$ is positive from opening 1
to opening 2.

The mean velocity, hydraulic diameter and channel
Reynolds number are
\begin{align}
	U_k &= \frac{q'_k}{H_k},
	\label{eq:channel_velocity}\\
	D_{h,k} &= 2H_k,
	\label{eq:hydraulic_diameter}\\
	Re_{h,k} &= \frac{\rho |U_k|D_{h,k}}{\mu}.
	\label{eq:channel_reynolds}
\end{align}
Here, $\rho$ and $\mu$ are the fluid density and
dynamic viscosity.

For steady, fully developed laminar flow, integration
of the axial momentum equation with no-slip conditions
at both walls gives the parallel-wall Hagen--Poiseuille
relation \cite{ShahLondon1978LaminarDucts}:
\begin{equation}
	\Delta p_{f,k}
	=
	R'_k |q'_k|
	=
	\frac{12\mu L_k}{H_k^3}|q'_k|.
	\label{eq:laminar_pressure_flow}
\end{equation}
The quantity $\Delta p_{f,k}\geq0$ is the magnitude
of the distributed frictional pressure drop.
The hydraulic resistance per unit span is
$R'_k=12\mu L_k/H_k^3$.
For fixed geometry and fluid properties, the laminar
frictional loss is therefore linear in the flow-rate
magnitude.

The frictional pressure drop is expressed more generally
using the Darcy--Weisbach relation
\cite{Moody1944FrictionFactors}:
\begin{equation}
	\Delta p_{f,k}
	=
	f_{D,k}
	\frac{L_k}{D_{h,k}}
	\frac{\rho U_k^2}{2},
	\label{eq:friction_drop}
\end{equation}
where $f_{D,k}$ is the Darcy friction factor.
For parallel-wall laminar flow,
$f_{D,k}=96/Re_{h,k}$.
Substitution into Eq.~\eqref{eq:friction_drop}
recovers Eq.~\eqref{eq:laminar_pressure_flow}.

A two-regime closure is adopted.
The laminar relation is used for $Re_{h,k}\leq2300$.
Above this threshold, the smooth-pipe Blasius correlation
is applied using the hydraulic diameter
\cite{Moody1944FrictionFactors}:
\begin{equation}
	f_{D,k}
	=
	\begin{cases}
		\displaystyle \frac{96}{Re_{h,k}},
		& 0<Re_{h,k}\leq2300,\\[6pt]
		\displaystyle 0.3164Re_{h,k}^{-1/4},
		& Re_{h,k}>2300.
	\end{cases}
	\label{eq:channel_friction_factor}
\end{equation}
Applying the Blasius correlation to the parallel-wall
geometry is an empirical approximation.
The value 2300 is a prescribed switching threshold.
No transition correlation or blending function is used.
The resulting frictional pressure--flow relation is
discontinuous at the threshold.

At zero flow, $\Delta p_{f,k}$ is set directly to zero.
This avoids evaluating the singular friction-factor
expression.
During hydraulic inversion, the Reynolds number is
recalculated from the candidate flow.
A candidate is accepted only when it satisfies the
channel energy equation and the Reynolds-number
condition of its selected regime.
If neither branch provides an admissible solution,
the inversion is rejected.

\subsection{Coupled formulation and nonlinear solution}
\label{subsec:coupled_solution}

The coupled unknowns are the signed channel flows
$\mathbf{q}=(q'_1,\ldots,q'_M)^{\mathsf T}$.
For each channel, the opening with the higher average
static pressure is selected as the inlet.
Its normal kinetic head contributes to the driving head.
Tangential kinetic energy is assumed to dissipate
during entry.

For a nonzero opening-pressure difference and a uniform
outlet jet ($\alpha_j=1$), the channel energy balance is
\begin{equation}
	\left|\Delta\overline{p}_k\right|
	+\frac{\rho}{2}
	\left\langle V_{n,\mathrm{in}}^2\right\rangle
	=
	\Delta p_{f,k}(Q_k)
	+\frac{\rho}{2}
	\left(\frac{Q_k}{H_k}\right)^2,
	\label{eq:channel_energy_equation}
\end{equation}
where
$\Delta\overline{p}_k=\overline{p}_{k,1}-\overline{p}_{k,2}$
and $Q_k=|q'_k|$.
The brackets denote the opening average defined in
Section~\ref{subsec:interface_transfer}.
The frictional loss $\Delta p_{f,k}$ follows
Section~\ref{subsec:internal_hydraulics}.
The outlet kinetic head is retained separately from
this loss.

For given opening pressures and inlet normal kinetic head,
hydraulic inversion determines the predicted flow magnitude.
Its direction follows the sign of
$\Delta\overline{p}_k$.
At equal opening pressures, the model prescribes zero flow.
Entrance turning and downstream jet mixing are not
resolved explicitly.

For fixed geometry, the linear aerodynamic system in
Eq.~\eqref{eq:aero_matrix} is reduced before solving
the channel-flow equations.
A single LU factorisation of $\mathbf{A}$ is used to obtain
\begin{equation}
	\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}_0=\mathbf{b}_{\infty},
	\qquad
	\mathbf{A}\mathbf{X}_q=\mathbf{B}.
	\label{eq:precomputed_aerodynamic_responses}
\end{equation}
Here, $\mathbf{x}_0$ is the impermeable-airfoil solution.
Each column of $\mathbf{X}_q$ gives the change in source
and vortex strengths caused by unit flow through one channel.
For any trial flow vector,
\begin{equation}
	\mathbf{x}
	=
	\mathbf{x}_0+\mathbf{X}_q\mathbf{q}.
	\label{eq:strength_reconstruction}
\end{equation}

The surface velocities follow as
\begin{align}
	\mathbf{V}_t
	&=
	\mathbf{V}_{t,0}+\mathbf{T}_q\mathbf{q},
	\label{eq:reduced_tangential_velocity}\\
	\mathbf{V}_n
	&=
	\mathbf{M}\mathbf{q},
	\label{eq:reduced_normal_velocity}
\end{align}
where
$\mathbf{V}_{t,0}=\mathbf{v}_{\infty,t}+\mathbf{C}\mathbf{x}_0$
and $\mathbf{T}_q=\mathbf{C}\mathbf{X}_q$.
The matrix $\mathbf{C}$ maps aerodynamic strengths
to induced tangential velocities.
The matrix $\mathbf{M}$ transfers channel flows
to the prescribed normal velocities.

These velocities are linear functions of the channel flows
plus the impermeable-airfoil contribution.
Bernoulli's equation therefore gives quadratic panel
pressures.
Opening averaging preserves this dependence.
The opening pressures and normal kinetic heads can thus
be evaluated using precomputed quadratic coefficients.
All terms in the discretised pressure relation are retained.
The reduction avoids repeated panel solves without
introducing a small-flow approximation.

The remaining nonlinear unknowns are the dimensionless
channel flows $\mathbf{z}$, defined by
\begin{equation}
	\mathbf{q}=\mathbf{S}\mathbf{z},
	\qquad
	\mathbf{S}
	=
	V_{\infty}\operatorname{diag}(H_1,\ldots,H_M).
	\label{eq:flow_scaling}
\end{equation}
For each trial $\mathbf{z}$, the reduced aerodynamic model
provides the opening pressures and inlet normal kinetic
heads.
Hydraulic inversion then gives the predicted flow vector
$\mathbf{q}_{\mathrm{hyd}}(\mathbf{z})$.
The coupled residual is
\begin{equation}
	\mathbf{R}(\mathbf{z})
	=
	\mathbf{z}
	-
	\mathbf{S}^{-1}\mathbf{q}_{\mathrm{hyd}}(\mathbf{z})
	=
	\mathbf{0}.
	\label{eq:coupled_residual}
\end{equation}
All channel flows are solved simultaneously because
they interact through the external pressure field.

A damped Newton method solves the residual equations\cite{NocedalWright2006NumericalOptimization}.
The analytical Jacobian is
\begin{equation}
	\mathbf{J}_R
	=
	\mathbf{I}_M
	-
	\mathbf{S}^{-1}
	\frac{\partial\mathbf{q}_{\mathrm{hyd}}}
	{\partial\mathbf{z}}.
	\label{eq:residual_jacobian}
\end{equation}
It is obtained by differentiating the quadratic aerodynamic
expressions and implicitly differentiating
Eq.~\eqref{eq:channel_energy_equation}.
The hydraulic derivatives include the dependence of
the friction factor on the channel Reynolds number.
Derivatives are evaluated within the selected inlet
and friction branches.

At iteration $n$, the Newton correction and flow update are
\begin{align}
	\mathbf{J}_R(\mathbf{z}^{(n)})
	\Delta\mathbf{z}
	&=
	-\mathbf{R}(\mathbf{z}^{(n)}),
	\label{eq:newton_system}\\
	\mathbf{z}^{(n+1)}
	&=
	\mathbf{z}^{(n)}
	+\eta_n\Delta\mathbf{z}.
	\label{eq:newton_update}
\end{align}
Armijo backtracking selects $0<\eta_n\leq1$ using the
merit function $\tfrac12\|\mathbf{R}\|_2^2$
\cite{NocedalWright2006NumericalOptimization}.
The full nonlinear residual is evaluated at every trial
update, including any changes in inlet or friction branch.
Ill-conditioned Newton systems are solved in a
least-squares sense.
A residual-gradient direction is used when needed
to obtain descent.

Initial flows are estimated from the impermeable-airfoil
pressure field or a nearby converged solution.
If Newton iterations fail to satisfy the residual tolerance,
a trust-region reflective least-squares method provides
a fallback \cite{Virtanen2020SciPy}.
It uses the same residual and analytical Jacobian.
A least-squares termination flag alone does not establish
a coupled solution.

Parameter sweeps may additionally use adaptive
angle-of-attack continuation, Reynolds-number continuation,
coupling homotopy and pseudo-arclength continuation
\cite{AllgowerGeorg2003NumericalContinuation}.
These procedures provide starting estimates for the
requested operating point.
Final acceptance uses a full panel solve and the original
hydraulic model.
The flow residual and channel energy errors must satisfy
the prescribed tolerances.
The flow direction and hydraulic regime must also be
admissible.
Only solutions at the requested operating conditions
and full aero--hydraulic coupling are accepted.


\subsection{Verification and aerodynamic coefficients}
\label{subsec:verification_outputs}

A converged reduced solution is independently evaluated
using a full panel solve and the original hydraulic
inversion. Acceptance requires a sufficiently small
dimensionless flow mismatch, a consistent channel energy
balance and an admissible flow direction. Failed cases
are recorded as nonconverged.

The pressure-force contribution of panel $i$ is
\begin{equation}
	\Delta C_{x,i}
	=
	-C_{p,i}n_{x,i}\frac{S_i}{c}.
	\label{eq:panel_x_force}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
	\Delta C_{y,i}
	=
	-C_{p,i}n_{y,i}\frac{S_i}{c}.
	\label{eq:panel_y_force}
\end{equation}
The total force coefficients are
\begin{equation}
	C_x=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\Delta C_{x,i}.
	\label{eq:total_x_force}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
	C_y=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\Delta C_{y,i}.
	\label{eq:total_y_force}
\end{equation}
Lift and drag coefficients follow by rotation through
the angle of attack $\alpha$:
\begin{equation}
	C_L=C_y\cos\alpha-C_x\sin\alpha.
	\label{eq:lift_coefficient}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
	C_D=C_x\cos\alpha+C_y\sin\alpha.
	\label{eq:drag_coefficient}
\end{equation}

Using a clockwise-positive moment convention, the moment
coefficient about $(x_{\mathrm{ref}},y_{\mathrm{ref}})$ is
\begin{equation}
	\begin{split}
		C_M=-\sum_{i=1}^{N}
		\Bigg[
		&\frac{x_i-x_{\mathrm{ref}}}{c}
		\Delta C_{y,i}
		\\
		&-
		\frac{y_i-y_{\mathrm{ref}}}{c}
		\Delta C_{x,i}
		\Bigg].
	\end{split}
	\label{eq:moment_coefficient}
\end{equation}

These coefficients represent integrated pressure loads
over the discretised panel boundary. They exclude
viscous shear and an explicit additional pore
momentum-flux reaction.
	
	\section{Results and discussion}
	\label{sec:results_discussion}
	
	The aerodynamic behaviour of the three porous-airfoil configurations
	was investigated over an angle-of-attack range of $-5^\circ$ to
	$15^\circ$. Three pore heights, $H=8$, $9$ and $10~\mathrm{mm}$,
	were considered for the horizontal, vertical and horizontal--vertical
	porous airfoils. The corresponding impermeable (solid) airfoil
	panel-method solution and the inviscid XFOIL prediction are included as
	reference cases.
	
	The effect of the pores is first examined through the lift and
	moment coefficients over the angle-of-attack range. The observed aerodynamic trends are then
	interpreted using the corresponding pressure and velocity field
	differences. A detailed flow-field comparison is presented at
	$\alpha=10^\circ$ for a pore height of $H=9~\mathrm{mm}$.
	
	
	% ============================================================
	% BASELINE VALIDATION
	% ============================================================
	
	\subsection{Panel method validation against XFOIL}
	\label{subsec:Panel_method_validation_against_XFOIL}
	
	The impermeable source--vortex panel-method solution is compared with
	the corresponding inviscid XFOIL prediction in
	Figures.~\ref{fig:cl_aoa_three_models} and
	\ref{fig:cm_aoa_three_models}. Both methods show similar trends in lift
	and pitching moment over the investigated angle-of-attack range.
	
	This agreement provides a useful reference for assessing the aerodynamic
	effects introduced by the pores. Small differences between the
	panel-method and XFOIL predictions are expected because the two
	approaches use different numerical formulations, although both represent
	inviscid external flow.
	
	The impermeable panel-method solution is therefore used as the primary
	reference when evaluating the porous-airfoil configurations. This
	ensures that the porous and solid airfoils are compared using the same
	geometry, panel discretisation, aerodynamic formulation and operating
	conditions.
	
	% ============================================================
	% LIFT
	% ============================================================
	
	\subsection{Effect of pores on lift}
	\label{subsec:lift_results}
	
	Figure~\ref{fig:cl_aoa_three_models} compares the variation of lift
	coefficient with angle of attack for the three porous-airfoil
	configurations and pore heights.
	\begin{figure*}[pos=!tp]
		\centering
		\includegraphics[
		width=0.96\textwidth
		]{figs/CL_vs_AoA_three_models_three_diameters.png}
		\caption{
			Lift coefficient as a function of angle of attack for
			(a) the horizontal porous airfoil,
			(b) the vertical porous airfoil, and
			(c) the horizontal--vertical porous airfoil.
			Results are shown for pore heights
			$H=8$, $9$ and $10~\mathrm{mm}$ together with the
			impermeable panel-method and inviscid XFOIL reference
			solutions.
		}
		\label{fig:cl_aoa_three_models}
	\end{figure*}
	The horizontal porous airfoil produces the largest change in lift.
	At positive angles of attack, its lift coefficient is higher than that
	of the impermeable reference, and the difference becomes more
	pronounced as the angle of attack increases. The effect also strengthens
	with pore height, with $H=10~\mathrm{mm}$ producing the highest lift
	at larger positive angles of attack. At negative angles of attack,
	increasing the pore height similarly increases the magnitude of the
	negative lift.This nearly symmetric lift response about the zero-lift condition is
	consistent with the symmetric NACA~0018 geometry.
	
	For the horizontal configuration, both ends of each pore are located on
	the same airfoil surface. The streamwise variation in surface pressure
	therefore creates a pressure difference between the two ends and drives
	internal flow from the locally higher-pressure region towards the
	lower-pressure region. This produces suction at one end and blowing at
	the other, changing the local surface-normal velocity and redistributing
	the external pressure and velocity fields along the airfoil surface.
	At positive angle of attack, the different pressure distributions over
	the suction and pressure surfaces lead to a net redistribution of
	aerodynamic loading that increases lift.
	
	The stronger dependence on pore height is consistent with the reduction
	in hydraulic resistance as the pore becomes larger. For a given pressure
	difference, a larger pore height permits a greater internal flow and
	therefore a stronger interaction between the pore flow and the external
	aerodynamic field.
	
	The vertical porous airfoil shows a distinctly different behaviour.
	At positive angles of attack, its lift coefficient is generally lower
	than that of the impermeable airfoil, while the three pore-height cases
	remain relatively close over most of the investigated range.
	
	In this configuration, each pore connects the  pressure
	surface to the suction surface. The resulting pressure
	difference drives internal flow between the two surfaces and partially
	reduces the pressure difference responsible for lift. This pressure
	equalisation is consistent with the lower lift coefficient obtained for
	the vertical porous airfoil. The small separation between the
	pore-height cases also indicates that this configuration is less
	sensitive to pore height than the horizontal arrangement.
	
	The horizontal--vertical porous airfoil remains comparatively close to
	the impermeable reference. At positive angles of attack, its lift
	coefficient is generally slightly lower than that of the solid airfoil,
	and the three pore-height cases remain closely grouped.
	
	This behaviour reflects the simultaneous influence of the horizontal
	and vertical pore arrangements. The horizontal pores redistribute flow
	between different chordwise locations on the same surface, whereas the
	vertical pores transfer flow between the pressure and suction surfaces.
	The lift-enhancing redistribution associated with the horizontal pores
	is therefore partly offset by the pressure equalisation introduced by
	the vertical pores.
	
	The horizontal and vertical pores remain physically separate and
	hydraulically independent. However, their aerodynamic effects interact
	through the external pressure field, since changes produced by one set
	of pores alter the surface pressures that drive the other set.
	Consequently, the response of the horizontal--vertical porous airfoil is
	not a simple superposition of the two individual configurations.
	
	Overall, the results show that the aerodynamic effect of the pores is
	governed mainly by their orientation, placement and the pressure
	difference between the connected surface locations. Pore height also
	influences the response, but its effect is strongest for the horizontal
	configuration and considerably weaker for the vertical and
	horizontal--vertical arrangements.
	% ============================================================
	% PITCHING MOMENT
	% ============================================================
	
	\subsection{Effect of pores on pitching moment}
	\label{subsec:moment_results}
	
	The corresponding moment coefficients are
	presented in Figure.~\ref{fig:cm_aoa_three_models}.
	\begin{figure*}[pos=!tp]
		\centering
		
		\includegraphics[
		width=0.96\textwidth
		]{figs/CM_vs_AoA_three_models_three_diameters.png}
		
		\caption{
			Pitching-moment coefficient about the quarter chord as a
			function of angle of attack for
			(a) the horizontal porous airfoil,
			(b) the vertical porous airfoil, and
			(c) the horizontal--vertical porous airfoil.
			Results are shown for pore heights
			$H=8$, $9$ and $10~\mathrm{mm}$ together with the
			impermeable panel-method and inviscid XFOIL reference solutions.
		}
		
		\label{fig:cm_aoa_three_models}
	\end{figure*}
	The horizontal porous airfoil produces the largest increase from the
	impermeable-airfoil response. As the positive angle of attack
	increases, the pitching-moment coefficient becomes progressively more
	negative. The magnitude of this change also increases with pore height,
	with $H=10~\mathrm{mm}$ producing the largest negative pitching moment
	at higher positive angles of attack.
	
	This behaviour is associated with the chordwise redistribution of
	surface pressure caused by the horizontal pores. Since the two ends
	of each pore are located at different chordwise positions along a given
	airfoil surface, the pressure changes induced by the internal flow act
	at different distances from the quarter-chord reference point. These
	local pressure variations therefore contribute differently to the
	pitching moment. Increasing the pore height strengthens the internal
	flow and the resulting pressure redistribution, leading to a larger
	change in the pitching-moment coefficient.
	
	The increase in lift obtained with the horizontal porous airfoil is
	therefore accompanied by a larger negative pitching moment. This
	represents an important aerodynamic trade-off, since the configuration
	that provides the greatest lift enhancement also produces the largest
	change in longitudinal loading.
	
	The vertical porous airfoil produces considerably smaller changes in
	moment, and the three pore-height cases remain closely grouped
	over most of the investigated angle-of-attack range. This behaviour is
	consistent with the weaker dependence on pore height observed in the
	lift results.
	
	For the vertical configuration, the two ends of each pore are positioned
	on the upper and lower surfaces at approximately the same chordwise
	location. The resulting pressure redistribution therefore occurs mainly
	between the pressure and suction surfaces rather than between widely
	separated positions along the chord. The corresponding change in the chordwise pressure distribution is
	smaller than for the horizontal pores, resulting in a weaker
	pitching-moment response.
	
	The horizontal--vertical porous airfoil exhibits behaviour between that
	of the individual horizontal and vertical configurations. The pitching
	moment becomes increasingly negative with positive angle of attack,
	although the departure from the impermeable-airfoil solution remains
	smaller than for the horizontal porous airfoil. The influence of pore
	height also becomes more noticeable at higher positive angles of attack.
	
	In this configuration, the horizontal pores redistribute loading
	between different chordwise positions, while the vertical pores mainly
	redistribute pressure between the upper and lower surfaces. The two pore
	arrangements remain physically separate and hydraulically independent,
	but their aerodynamic effects interact through the external pressure
	field. The resulting pitching-moment variation therefore reflects the
	combined influence of these two pressure-redistribution mechanisms.
	
	Overall, the pitching-moment results show that the effect of the pores
	depends not only on the extent of the pressure redistribution, but also
	on where it occurs along the chord. The horizontal porous airfoil
	produces the largest change in moment because the associated
	pressure variations occur at widely separated chordwise locations,
	creating a stronger moment about the quarter-chord reference point.
	In contrast, the vertical porous airfoil produces a smaller change
	because its pressure redistribution occurs mainly between the upper
	and lower surfaces at similar chordwise positions.
	
	
	% ============================================================
	% FLOW FIELD
	% ============================================================
	
	\subsection{Flow field characteristics of porous airfoils}
	\label{subsec:flowfield_results}
	
	To examine how the pores alter the external flow, the pressure and
	velocity fields of the porous airfoils are compared with those of the
	corresponding impermeable airfoil at $\alpha=10^\circ$ and
	$H=9~\mathrm{mm}$.
	
	The comparison is presented using pressure- and velocity-difference
	contours rather than the absolute flow fields. The absolute contours
	are dominated by the overall aerodynamic behaviour of the airfoil and
	therefore make the local influence of the pores more difficult to
	identify. Subtracting the impermeable-airfoil solution isolates the
	changes introduced by the pore flow, allowing the location, direction
	and relative strength of the resulting pressure and velocity variations
	to be examined more clearly.
	
	
	% ============================================================
	% PRESSURE FIELD
	% ============================================================
	
	\subsubsection{Pressure field characteristics}
	
	Figure~\ref{fig:pressure_difference} presents the pressure difference
	between the porous and impermeable airfoils,
	
	\begin{equation}
		\Delta p
		=
		p_{\mathrm{porous}}
		-
		p_{\mathrm{solid}},
		\label{eq:pressure_field_difference}
	\end{equation}
	
	for the three pore arrangements.
	
	\begin{figure*}[pos=!tp]
		\centering
		\includegraphics[
		width=0.62\textwidth
		]{figs/pressure_difference_pa_portrait_one_colorbar.png}
		\caption{{\normalfont\normalsize
				Pressure-field difference between the porous and
				impermeable airfoils at $\alpha=10^\circ$ and
				$H=9~\mathrm{mm}$ for
				(a) the horizontal porous airfoil,
				(b) the vertical porous airfoil, and
				(c) the horizontal--vertical porous airfoil.
				Positive values indicate an increase in pressure relative
				to the impermeable airfoil, while negative values indicate
				a reduction.
		}}
		\label{fig:pressure_difference}
	\end{figure*}
	
	The pressure difference is shown instead of the absolute pressure field
	to isolate the changes introduced by the pores. In the absolute
	solution, the pressure distribution is dominated by the overall
	airfoil loading and the strong pressure gradients near the leading
	edge. Subtracting the impermeable-airfoil solution makes the local
	changes associated with the pore flow easier to identify.
	
	The strongest pressure differences occur near the pore ends, where the
	internal flow directly influences the surrounding external flow. In
	several locations, neighbouring positive and negative values of
	$\Delta p$ form a dipole-like pattern. This is associated with suction
	at one end of a pore and blowing at the other, producing local regions
	of reduced and increased pressure relative to the impermeable airfoil.
	
	The effect of these pressure changes on lift depends on where they occur
	on the airfoil. At positive angle of attack, a reduction in pressure on
	the upper suction surface strengthens the suction and contributes to
	higher lift, whereas an increase in pressure has the opposite effect.
	On the lower pressure surface, an increase in pressure contributes
	positively to lift, while a pressure reduction tends to decrease it.
	
	For the horizontal porous airfoil, the pressure differences are mainly
	concentrated around the chordwise pore ends. Because both ends of each
	pore lie on the same airfoil surface, the internal flow redistributes
	pressure between different chordwise locations. The combined pressure
	changes on the upper and lower surfaces increase the pressure difference
	across the airfoil, consistent with the higher lift coefficient observed
	for this configuration.
	
	The vertical porous airfoil produces a different pattern because each
	pore connects the pressure surface to the suction surface. Flow is
	therefore driven from the higher-pressure region towards the
	lower-pressure region, producing local pressure changes at both pore
	ends. At the same time, this cross-surface flow partially reduces the
	pressure difference between the two surfaces, which is consistent with
	the lower lift coefficient obtained for the vertical configuration.
	
	The horizontal--vertical porous airfoil contains features associated
	with both pore arrangements. The horizontal pores redistribute pressure
	along the airfoil surfaces, while the vertical pores transfer flow
	between the pressure and suction surfaces. Although the two pore systems
	remain physically separate and hydraulically independent, their
	aerodynamic effects interact through the external pressure field.
	Consequently, the pressure equalisation associated with the vertical
	pores partly counteracts the pressure redistribution produced by the
	horizontal pores, giving an overall aerodynamic behaviour closer to
	that of the impermeable airfoil.

	% VELOCITY FIELD
	% ============================================================
	
	\subsubsection{Velocity field characteristics}
	
	\begin{figure*}[pos=!tp]
		\centering
		\includegraphics[
		width=0.62\textwidth
		]{figs/velocity_difference_percent_portrait_one_colorbar.png}
		\caption{{\normalfont\normalsize
				Percentage difference in velocity magnitude between the porous
				and impermeable airfoils at $\alpha=10^\circ$ and
				$H=9~\mathrm{mm}$ for
				(a) the horizontal porous airfoil,
				(b) the vertical porous airfoil, and
				(c) the horizontal--vertical porous airfoil.
				Positive values indicate an increase in velocity magnitude,
				while negative values indicate a reduction relative to the
				impermeable airfoil.
		}}
		\label{fig:velocity_difference}
	\end{figure*}
	
	The velocity field is presented as the percentage difference relative
	to the impermeable-airfoil solution,
	
	\begin{equation}
		\Delta |\boldsymbol{V}|_{\%}
		=
		\frac{
			|\boldsymbol{V}|_{\mathrm{porous}}
			-
			|\boldsymbol{V}|_{\mathrm{solid}}
		}{
			|\boldsymbol{V}|_{\mathrm{solid}}
		}
		\times 100.
		\label{eq:velocity_field_difference}
	\end{equation}
	
	This comparison isolates the changes introduced by the pores from the dominant background flow around the airfoil. The largest velocity differences occur close to the pore ends,
	where the imposed surface-normal flow locally perturbs the external
	velocity field. Adjacent regions of acceleration and deceleration are
	visible around several pores and correspond closely to the pressure
	patterns shown in Figure.~\ref{fig:pressure_difference}.
	
	The velocity and pressure fields show the expected inverse relationship
	described by Bernoulli's equation. Regions where the flow accelerates
	are generally associated with a reduction in static pressure, whereas
	regions of lower velocity correspond to higher static pressure. The
	velocity contours therefore help explain how the pore-induced changes
	in the local flow field lead to the observed pressure redistribution
	and, consequently, changes in the aerodynamic forces and pitching
	moment.
	
	For the horizontal porous airfoil, alternating regions of acceleration
	and deceleration are concentrated around the chordwise pore ends. Since
	both ends of each pore lie on the same airfoil surface, the internal
	flow redistributes the external velocity between different chordwise
	locations. The combined changes on the suction and pressure surfaces
	strengthen the pressure difference across the airfoil, consistent with
	the increase in lift and the larger change in pitching moment observed
	for this configuration.
	
	The vertical porous airfoil shows a different velocity pattern. Flow
	between the pressure and suction surfaces changes the external velocity
	on both sides of the airfoil and reduces part of the velocity contrast
	between them. This behaviour is consistent with the partial pressure
	equalisation discussed previously and the corresponding reduction in
	lift. A broader region of velocity difference is also visible near the
	leading edge, where the external flow experiences strong pressure and
	velocity gradients.
	
	The horizontal--vertical porous airfoil contains features associated
	with both pore arrangements. The horizontal pores produce local
	chordwise acceleration and deceleration, while the vertical pores
	redistribute flow between the pressure and suction surfaces. Although
	the two pore arrangements remain physically separate and hydraulically
	independent, their aerodynamic effects interact through the external
	flow field. The resulting velocity distribution therefore reflects the
	combined influence of both mechanisms rather than a simple
	superposition of the individual configurations.
	
	Overall, the velocity contours support the trends observed in the
	pressure field and aerodynamic coefficients. The horizontal pores
	primarily redistribute the flow along the airfoil surfaces, whereas the
	vertical pores transfer flow between the pressure and suction surfaces.
	These different flow mechanisms contribute to the contrasting lift and
	pitching-moment behaviour of the three porous-airfoil configurations.
	
	
	
	\section{Conclusion and future work}
	\label{sec:conclusion}
	
	A reduced-order aero--hydraulic framework has been developed to investigate
	NACA~0018 airfoils containing pressure-driven internal channels. The external
	flow is represented using a source--vortex panel method and is coupled to
	finite-length rectangular-channel models through the pore-normal velocity.
	The main conclusions of the present study are:
	
	\begin{itemize}
		
		\item The aerodynamic response of a porous airfoil depends strongly on
		the orientation, location and connectivity of the internal channels.
		Porosity alone is therefore insufficient to describe the resulting
		aerodynamic behaviour.
		
		\item Horizontal chordwise channels produce the strongest aerodynamic
		modification among the configurations considered. By connecting
		different chordwise locations on the same airfoil surface, these channels
		redistribute pressure without directly equalising the pressure and
		suction surfaces.
		
		\item At positive angles of attack, the horizontal configuration
		increases lift relative to the impermeable airfoil. The magnitude of this
		effect increases with channel height, demonstrating the sensitivity of
		the aerodynamic response to the hydraulic resistance of the internal
		passages.
		
		\item The lift enhancement produced by the horizontal channels is
		accompanied by a more negative pitching moment. This demonstrates that
		improvements in lift cannot be considered independently of changes in
		longitudinal aerodynamic loading.
		
		\item Vertical channels exhibit a fundamentally different behaviour.
		Connecting the pressure and suction surfaces allows pressure-driven flow
		between the two sides of the airfoil, producing partial pressure
		equalisation and consequently reducing lift relative to the impermeable
		configuration at positive angles of attack.
		
		\item The combined horizontal--vertical configuration does not behave as
		a simple superposition of the two individual arrangements. Although the
		channel systems are hydraulically independent, they interact through the
		external pressure field. The pressure equalisation associated with the
		vertical channels therefore partly offsets the lift-enhancing effect of
		the horizontal channels.
		
		\item Pressure- and velocity-difference fields show that the strongest
		aerodynamic modifications occur locally around the channel openings.
		Suction and blowing generated by the internal flow alter the surrounding
		external velocity and pressure fields, which subsequently modify the
		integrated aerodynamic forces and pitching moment.
		
		\item The results demonstrate that the pressure difference between
		connected surface locations is a key driver of porous-airfoil behaviour.
		Channel placement, orientation and height should therefore be considered
		simultaneously when designing internal porous networks.
		
		\item The developed framework provides a computationally inexpensive
		method for screening multiple internal-channel configurations before
		progressing to substantially more expensive viscous CFD simulations or
		experimental investigations.
		
	\end{itemize}
	
	The present results should be interpreted as inviscid aerodynamic trends.
	The external panel method does not resolve boundary layers, transition,
	skin-friction drag, flow separation or stall, and the pressure-based
	streamwise-force coefficient should therefore not be interpreted as the
	total drag of the porous airfoil. These limitations define the next stage
	of the investigation.
	
	\subsection{Future work}
	
	Future development will focus on:
	
	\begin{itemize}
		
		\item incorporating a viscous boundary-layer model into the panel-method
		framework to estimate skin-friction drag, transition and the influence
		of viscous--inviscid interaction;
		
		\item extending the design space beyond the three prescribed
		configurations by optimising channel location, orientation, height and
		connectivity to identify improved internal porous-network topologies;
		
		\item investigating selected configurations using higher-fidelity
		unsteady CFD to resolve pore-mouth flow, boundary-layer interaction,
		separation and wake dynamics that cannot be represented by the present
		reduced-order formulation;
		
		\item validating the predicted pressure redistribution and aerodynamic
		trends experimentally using manufactured porous-airfoil models; and
		
		\item extending the analysis towards three-dimensional porous wings and
		application-relevant operating conditions once the underlying
		aero--hydraulic mechanisms have been established.
		
	\end{itemize}
	
	The present framework therefore provides the first stage of a hierarchical
	design process in which rapid reduced-order screening is used to identify
	promising internal-channel arrangements for subsequent optimisation,
	high-fidelity viscous simulation and experimental validation.
